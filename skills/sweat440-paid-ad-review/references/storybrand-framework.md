# SWEAT440 StoryBrand 2.0 Framework

StoryBrand is the narrative spine of every piece of SWEAT440 primary text and every
creative brief. The five messaging pillars are the emotional content; StoryBrand is
the structure that carries that content forward. Read this alongside
`pillars-and-personas.md` — the pillar names the internal problem and transformation,
the StoryBrand beats order the copy.

## The One Rule That Outranks Everything

**The reader is the hero. SWEAT440 is the guide — never the hero.**

Brand-as-hero framing is the highest-priority StoryBrand flag. Any copy that positions
SWEAT440 as the protagonist of the story is a failure, even if every other standard is
met. Rewrite it so the reader is the one who moves forward and changes.

Flag and rewrite on sight:
- "We built the fix" → "You finally have a way through"
- "We're the best workout in the city" → "The workout that finally fits your life"
- "Our coaches are elite" → "A coach who has you the moment you walk in"
- "We created a system that works" → "A system that does the thinking, so you just show up"

A simple test: if the sentence brags about SWEAT440, it is brand-as-hero. If it shows
the reader winning, it is guide-positioned. The brand earns trust by making the reader
the winner.

## The Seven Beats

Every primary text should move through these beats in order. Not every beat needs its
own sentence — short ad copy can collapse several into one line — but the arc must be
present and in sequence.

### 1. A Character (the hero)
Open on the reader and what they want. The want is concrete and tied to the persona,
not generic fitness ("get in shape"). Name the version of themselves they are reaching
for.

### 2. Has a Problem (lead with the internal problem)
SWEAT440 sells against an **internal** problem, not an external one. The external
problem is "I want to work out." The internal problem is how the barrier *feels* — and
that feeling is the pillar. Always name the internal problem before any feature.

| Pillar | External problem | Internal problem (lead with this) |
|--------|------------------|-----------------------------------|
| P1 — Psychological Safety | "I should exercise" | "I'm afraid of being watched and judged" |
| P2 — Decision Relief | "I don't have a plan" | "I'm exhausted from deciding everything" |
| P3 — Social Safety & Belonging | "I'd go to a gym" | "I don't want to walk in alone and feel like a number" |
| P4 — Value Confidence | "It costs money" | "What if I pay and don't go — again" |
| P5 — Performance & Results (earned) | "I want results" | "I'm tired of effort that goes nowhere" |

The philosophical problem (the "this is just wrong" layer) is optional and usually
implicit: it shouldn't be this hard to start, you shouldn't have to choose between your
life and your health.

### 3. And Meets a Guide (empathy before authority)
SWEAT440 enters here as the guide, and **empathy must come before authority**. First
show you understand the feeling ("we get why you've been putting this off"), then —
and only then — establish credibility (coaching, format, the 10-minute start). Copy
that leads with proof or credentials before empathy is inverted; flag it.

- Empathy = "we understand where you are." (warmth)
- Authority = the proof point that you can actually guide them. (competence)
- Order is always empathy → authority.

### 4. Who Gives Them a Plan (a simple plan before the CTA)
The reader will not act until the path looks simple. Show a short, concrete plan —
ideally three steps — before asking for the click. The plan removes the fear of the
unknown.

SWEAT440's default process plan:
1. Book your first class (a class starts every 10 minutes)
2. Show up — the coach takes it from there
3. Walk out having done it

If the copy jumps from problem straight to CTA with no plan, flag it: the reader has
nothing to lower the activation barrier.

### 5. And Calls Them to Action
One clear call to action. Follow the CTA standards in `standing-standards.md` — match
the action phrase to the pillar and persona ("just show up" for P1/P3, "see what a
real system delivers" for P5). Direct CTA for warm audiences; a softer transitional
CTA (learn / see how it works) can precede it for cold traffic.

### 6. That Helps Them Avoid Failure (the stakes — used with restraint)
Name what staying stuck costs, but stay on-pillar and never use friction language.
Stakes are the quiet undertow, not the headline. Keep it to a light touch — one line
at most — and never tip into "no excuses," shame, or manufactured urgency (all banned
in `standing-standards.md`). The stakes are about the cost of *staying the same*, not
about the reader having failed.

### 7. And Ends in Success (close on transformation, not the offer)
End on **who the reader becomes**, not on a feature or the deal. The transformation is
the aspirational identity the pillar promises. The offer can appear, but it is never
the emotional close.

| Pillar | Transformation (close on this) |
|--------|-------------------------------|
| P1 | "Someone who walks in like they belong — because they do" |
| P2 | "Someone who just shows up and lets the plan carry them" |
| P3 | "Someone who has a place, and people who notice when they're there" |
| P4 | "Someone whose membership finally pays off, every week" |
| P5 | "Someone whose progress is planned, not accidental" |

## One Pillar, Seven Beats

The seven beats are the structure; the pillar is the single emotional throughline that
runs through all of them. **One pillar per creative still applies.** The internal
problem (beat 2) and the transformation (beat 7) must be the *same* pillar — if the
copy opens on P1 fear-of-judgment but closes on P5 results, the arc has switched
pillars and breaks. Flag any creative whose problem and transformation come from
different pillars.

Sequencing still governs: P5 may only carry the arc for warm audiences or the Parents
with Older Children persona (see `pillars-and-personas.md`). For cold P5, the
transformation beat must be borrowed from the trust pillar that earns it.

## Worked Example (P1 — Single Individuals, cold Meta Feed)

> **Hero + internal problem:** You've thought about walking in longer than you'd admit.
> Not because you're lazy — because every room felt like it was watching.
> **Guide, empathy first:** We get it. So we built classes where there's no spotlight
> to stand in. **Authority:** Coach-led stations, a set format, nowhere to wander and
> feel exposed. **Plan:** Book a class, show up, the coach has you from the door.
> **Transformation:** You stop being the person outside the glass. You're just someone
> who trains here now. **CTA:** Just show up — your first class is on us.

Why it passes: reader is hero throughout, internal problem (P1 fear of judgment) leads,
empathy precedes authority, a three-step plan lowers the barrier, and the close is an
identity shift, not the offer. One pillar (P1) start to finish.

## StoryBrand Review Checklist

Run on every primary text and every brief:

1. **Hero check** — is the reader the hero, or is SWEAT440 bragging about itself?
   (brand-as-hero = highest-priority flag)
2. **Internal problem** — does the copy name how the barrier *feels*, mapped to the
   stated pillar, before any feature?
3. **Empathy before authority** — does understanding come before proof, in that order?
4. **Plan present** — is there a simple (ideally 3-step) path shown before the CTA?
5. **Transformation close** — does it end on who the reader becomes, not a feature or
   the offer?
6. **One pillar** — do the internal problem (beat 2) and transformation (beat 7) come
   from the same pillar?
7. **No banned friction** — stakes stay light, on-pillar, and free of "no excuses,"
   shame, or manufactured urgency.

Every StoryBrand flag, like all flags in this skill, must come with a specific rewrite.
